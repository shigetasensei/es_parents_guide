Four ideas that show up in almost every expert source

  1. Connection beats control. Kids tell you more when they expect calm, not punishment.
  2. Quality, context and what it replaces matter more than a single magic number of minutes.
  3. Co-use early. Watch, play and explore with your child, then give independence in steps.
  4. Plans beat arguments. Agree on routines in calm moments, and revisit them as kids grow.

🤖 Trail 1

AI in Learning & Life

Your child will grow up with AI in their search tools, homework apps and games. The goal isn't to fear it or to hand over the keys — it's to build understanding, curiosity and good judgment.

Big ideas

What AI actually is (say it to your child like this)

Most of today's AI finds patterns in huge amounts of data and uses them to predict or generate something — a recommended video, a face match, the next word in a sentence. Chatbots are trained to produce text that sounds right. That's different from knowing, understanding or caring.

The AI4K12 initiative (AAAI & CSTA) frames school-age AI literacy around five big ideas: perception, representation & reasoning, learning, natural interaction, and societal impact. Elementary kids can explore all five through play.

Confident doesn't mean correct

Generative AI can state wrong facts — or invent books, quotes and links — in a calm, authoritative voice. Build one habit early: "Who says so, and how can we check?" Try the Fact-check Lab below.

Humans stay in charge

The U.S. Department of Education's 2023 report on AI and teaching centers on keeping humans in the loop — teachers, parents and students making the decisions, with AI as a tool. UNESCO's 2023 guidance on generative AI similarly stresses a human-centered approach, teacher involvement, and protecting learners' data and wellbeing.

Homework: learning is the point

The test for any AI use: "Am I still doing the thinking?" Explaining, quizzing and brainstorming can support learning. Having AI produce the work to hand in replaces it. Schools differ — check your school's AI policy and keep teachers in the loop. IB schools, for example, expect any AI help in assessed work to be acknowledged (see below).

What the IB says about AI (for IB-school families)

The International Baccalaureate (IB) chose not to ban AI tools such as ChatGPT. Its 2023 statement describes the technology as an opportunity, compares it with spellcheckers and calculators, and says students still need skills such as judging whether a piece of writing is good. Under the IB's academic integrity policy, which now includes guidance on AI tools:

  • Work produced by AI tools, even in part, is not considered the student's own work.
  • AI-generated text, images or graphs used in assessed work must be credited in the body of the work and listed in the bibliography. The IB frames this as transparency.
  • Schools choose the referencing style, and not acknowledging a source can be investigated as a potential breach of the IB's regulations.

日本語：IBはAIツールを禁止していません。AIが作った内容は（一部でも）生徒自身の作品とは見なされず、評価される課題でAIの生成物を使う場合は、本文中で示し、参考文献にも記載します（透明性の原則）。引用の形式は各学校が決めます。

These statements are written mainly for work assessed by the IB, not for young children's everyday learning. No separate IB position specific to the Primary Years Programme was found, so ask your school how it applies these principles in the primary years. The IB updates its policies; read the current wording on ibo.org.

Privacy & age limits

Anything typed or uploaded may be stored or used by the service. Teach "no names, school, address, photos of people, or secrets." Many general-purpose chatbots set a minimum age of 13 in their terms (some require a parent's permission under 18), and UNESCO noted 13 as a commonly used threshold while saying many experts think it's too young. Read each tool's terms; supervised, child-specific tools are a better fit for younger kids.

AI "friends" and companions

Common Sense Media's risk assessment of AI companion apps concluded they are not suitable for anyone under 18, because they can produce inappropriate content, are built to be agreeable, and aren't equipped for serious emotional conversations. Keep your child's emotional support human: parents, family, teachers, counselors.

Fakes, bias and "is this real?"

AI can create realistic images, voices and video, and it can reflect biases in its training data. For elementary kids: "Pictures and videos can be edited or made by computers. If it's surprising, pause and ask."

⚖️ Trail 2

Media Balance

Screens aren't all good or all bad. The question is what the screen is doing for your child — and what it's pushing out.

Big ideas

From "how many minutes?" to "how, what and when?"

The American Academy of Pediatrics' January 2026 policy statement, Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents, moves beyond simple screen-time limits. It looks at platform design (many are built to maximize engagement and profit), the quality of content, the relationships around use, and what media displaces. It calls for safer defaults like autoplay off and no targeted ads to minors — and still encourages families to make a plan.

Protect the big three: sleep, movement, people

Guidelines suggest roughly 9–11 hours of sleep for ages 5–13 (Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines; the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's consensus is 9–12 hours for ages 6–12), about 60 minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous activity (Canada, WHO), and — in Canada's and Australia's guidelines — no more than about 2 hours of recreational screen time per day. Treat that last number as a reference point, not a verdict; the displacement question matters more.

The "5 Cs" check

The AAP's "5 Cs of media use" framework, associated with pediatrician Dr. Jenny Radesky, suggests asking: Child (how is mine doing?), Content (is it good?), Calm (can they stop without a meltdown?), Crowding out (what's it replacing?), and Communication (are we talking about it?). Try the Quality Check below.

Bedtime and bedrooms

The AAP has long recommended keeping devices out of bedrooms overnight and turning screens off about an hour before bed. Charging devices in a shared space makes that easier for everyone — grown-ups included.

Design tricks that make stopping hard

Autoplay, streaks, infinite scroll and reward loops are designed to keep people watching. Telling kids "this was built to be hard to stop" turns a power struggle into a shared puzzle. The U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on social media and youth mental health makes the same point about teens' use; elementary years are the time to build awareness before the pull gets stronger.

Model it

Kids notice when a phone comes out at dinner. Agree on grown-up screen-free times too — it's fairer, and it works better.

🛡️ Trail 3

Internet Safety

Tools help, but the strongest safety feature is a child who knows they can tell you anything and won't lose the device — or your trust — for doing so.

Canadian Academy · 神戸

The CA Way: Digital

At school, CA students follow The CA Way: be Safe, Respectful and Responsible. Here is the Digital row, with ideas for practicing it at home. Use the same words with your child, so school and home sound alike.

Safe

We use our bodies and materials in expected ways.

Protect and do not share yours or someone else's YAPPY.

At home: YAPPY is the CA reminder of what to keep private online: Your (full) name, Address, Password, Phone number and Your plan. Say it together, and practice with the Privacy lab.

Tell an adult if you see something inappropriate online.

At home: Say it out loud: "You will never be in trouble for telling me." Play Go, Pause or Stop & Tell together, and keep the help finder handy.

Respectful

We use kind words and actions and follow teacher directions.

Only access your own profiles and content.

At home: Passwords are only for you and your parents. No logging in to a friend's account or opening someone else's files, even as a joke. The passphrase practice helps kids make their own.

Use kind, caring and positive words.

At home: Ask "Would I say this out loud, to their face?" before sending. If someone is unkind to your child online, see what to do.

Responsible

We take care of ourselves and our school.

Stay on the expected websites for each lesson.

At home: Decide the apps and sites before starting, and use kid-safe profiles. Say what "done" looks like, then stop. A Family Media Plan makes this easy.

Use devices carefully so they function properly.

At home: Treat devices like borrowed tools: handle them gently, and charge them in a shared place overnight (it also protects sleep).

Big ideas

"Go, Pause, Stop & Tell"

A simple script kids can remember: Go = fine. Pause = check with a grown-up first. Stop & tell = don't engage, leave or close it, tell a trusted adult. Practice it with the game below.

Tell-us-and-you're-not-in-trouble

NCMEC (the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children), through its NetSmartz program, and StopBullying.gov both stress responding calmly and making sure children know it isn't their fault. If kids fear losing devices, they hide problems. Say it out loud, often: "If something online feels weird, tell me. You will not be in trouble."

Where kids actually meet strangers

It's not only social media. Multiplayer game chat, comment sections, and shared creative platforms let unknown people reach children. Look at who can message your child and set chat to friends-only or off where possible.

Age minimums and privacy law

In the U.S., the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) limits how online services collect personal information from children under 13, which is a main reason most big social platforms set 13 as their minimum age. Age checks are easy to bypass, so don't rely on them — treat the minimum as guidance and decide as a family.

Scams, secrets and "special friends"

Warning signs worth teaching: requests for personal info, offers that are "free," pressure to move to another app, asking for pictures, and any request to keep a secret from parents. Online enticement and sextortion are most often reported with teens, but the groundwork — body privacy, "no secrets from parents," telling early — starts in the elementary years (see NCMEC's sextortion tip sheet for parents).

Kindness & cyberbullying

StopBullying.gov's advice: don't retaliate, save evidence (screenshots), block and report, and tell a trusted adult or the school. Ask "How would it feel to get this message?" before sending.

Parental controls: useful, not magic

Built-in tools like Apple Screen Time, Google Family Link and Microsoft Family Safety can set content ratings, approve downloads and manage time. Kids find workarounds, so pair them with conversation and shared spaces.

🌱 Preschool & kindergarten · ages 3–5

The early years

At this age, the best "learning technology" is still sleep, play, books and you. Screens can have a small, well-chosen place — and the habits you set now (who chooses, where, when and how it ends) carry into elementary school.

The daily picture at ages 3–4

Whole-day guidelines from the WHO and Canada's early-years guidelines (the same numbers; Canada's cover ages 0–4). Think of them as reference points, not a pass/fail test.

10–13 hsleep a day, naps included
180 minof physical activity, of which 60 min energetic play
≤ 1 hof screen time (sitting), less is better
Every dayreading, singing, talking and pretend play with you

Sources: WHO (2019) and CSEP (2017) for movement, screens and sleep; AASM (2016) also suggests 10–13 hours for ages 3–5. The AAP's earlier guidance for ages 2–5 was about 1 hour a day of high-quality programming; its 2026 policy statement focuses more on content quality, platform design and context.

🇯🇵 In Japan, MEXT's Early Childhood Physical Activity Guideline (幼児期運動指針) suggests at least 60 minutes of active movement each day, mainly through play. See the Japan page.

Big ideas

Play and people come first

Young children learn through back-and-forth conversation, pretend play, movement, books and routines. A screen can fit around those things, but shouldn't push them out. If you are not sure whether something is a good choice, ask: "What would we be doing instead?"

Quality and co-viewing beat minutes alone

The American Academy of Pediatrics' earlier guidance for ages 2–5 advised choosing high-quality programming, watching together, and helping children connect what they see to real life. Look for slow pacing, a clear story or learning goal, no ads, no pressure to keep going, and an obvious ending.

Turn off background TV

The AAP advises against leaving the TV on in the background when no one is watching, because it can take attention away from play and from conversation. Make "TV on" a decision, not a default.

Not the only way to calm down

The AAP cautions against using media as the only way to calm a child. A study led by Dr. Jenny Radesky of 3–5-year-olds (JAMA Pediatrics, 2023) found that frequent use of devices to calm children was associated with more emotional reactivity over the next six months. It is an association, not proof of cause, and an occasional screen in a hard moment is part of real life. The idea is to also teach other ways to calm down: breathing, hugs, a calm corner, a song.

Bedtime, bedrooms and routines

Ten to thirteen hours of sleep (naps included) is the target for this age. The AAP suggests turning screens off about an hour before bed and keeping devices out of bedrooms overnight. A predictable order (bath, teeth, pajamas, book, lights out) works better than a negotiation each night.

Making "all done" easier

Preschoolers find it very hard to stop something fun. Decide before starting what and for how long, use a visible timer, give a 5-minute and then a 1-minute warning, and have a next activity ready ("Now we are going to make lunch together"). Stay calm and kind, and keep the rule the same each time.

Safe use: you are the filter

Children this age should not be alone on the open internet. Use kids-only apps or profiles, turn off autoplay, chat and in-app purchases, and choose the content yourself. Open video sites can lead from one video to another in a few taps. Keep devices in shared spaces.

Talking gadgets and AI

Voice assistants, smart toys and video recommendations all use AI. Young children may treat talking devices as if they were alive. Say it simply and often: "It's a computer, not a person." AI can also be wrong. General-purpose chatbots and AI companion apps aren't designed for preschoolers (many set a minimum age of 13, and Common Sense Media's risk assessment says AI companions are not suitable for under-18s). If you use a voice assistant, do it together, with child settings on.

Privacy and sharing your child's photos

Your child can't consent to being posted. Share photos privately with family, leave out school names, uniforms and locations, and ask relatives to do the same. Teach the first safety rule early: "My body belongs to me, and I never keep secrets from my grown-ups."

Age by age

Every child develops at their own pace. These are starting points.

Age 3

  • Short, parent-chosen shows, watched together; keep it simple and slow.
  • Many 3-year-olds still need naps. Protect them.
  • Lots of "why?" Questions are a chance to talk, not just search.
  • Routines and a visible timer help with "all done".

Age 4

  • Can start learning simple rules ("When the timer rings, we turn it off").
  • Introduce "ads want you to want things" in simple words.
  • Start the body-safety words and the "no secrets" rule.
  • Let your child teach you a game; it builds talking and pride.

Age 5 / kindergarten

  • School may introduce tablets or computers. Ask the teacher how they are used.
  • Practice Go / Pause / Stop & tell with pretend situations.
  • Still 10–13 hours of sleep, and plenty of running around.
  • Keep devices in shared spaces, with no open chat or open video sites.

What Japan's experts and ministries say

Japan's pediatric organizations and ministries (厚生労働省 MHLW, 文部科学省 MEXT, こども家庭庁 Children and Families Agency) publish their own guidance on sleep, movement, screens, AI and online safety. Here it is next to the international advice, with the Japanese names so you can find the originals, plus a directory of help lines in Japan.

What each organization says 各機関の見解

Japan Pediatric Association 日本小児科医会

In 2004 the association's "Children and Media" committee issued five recommendations (「子どもとメディア」の問題に対する提言) and has kept running poster and leaflet campaigns since, including "Don't let the smartphone babysit" (スマホに子守りをさせないで). Its key points: avoid TV and video up to age 2; turn them off during feeding and meals; keep total time with all media to about 2 hours a day, and video games to about 30 minutes; keep TVs and computers out of children's rooms; and talk with your child and read picture books together.

日本語：2歳までのテレビ・ビデオ視聴を控える／授乳中・食事中はテレビ・ビデオを消す／メディア総接触時間は1日2時間まで、ゲームは30分まで／子ども部屋にテレビ・パソコンを置かない／語りかけと絵本の読み聞かせ。

These are older, time-based guidelines. Not every organization sets the same limits, and newer international guidance puts more weight on content and context. A recent, separate statement on phones and tablets from the Japan Pediatric Society (日本小児科学会) was not found; check its site for its current position.

Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare 厚生労働省

Sleep Guide 2023 (健康づくりのための睡眠ガイド2023) recommends 9–12 hours for elementary school children and 8–10 hours for junior and senior high school students, and encourages morning light, a good breakfast, daytime exercise and avoiding late nights. It notes that ages should not be applied rigidly and individual needs differ. For younger children, official and prefectural materials cite the American Academy of Sleep Medicine ranges (ages 1–2: 11–14 h; 3–5: 10–13 h).

Physical Activity Guide 2023 (健康づくりのための身体活動・運動ガイド2023) suggests children average 60 minutes a day of moderate-or-harder activity (WHO-based), with strengthening and high-intensity activity on three or more days a week, and recommends reducing sitting time, especially recreational screen time. It does not give a numeric screen-time limit.

日本語：睡眠は小学生9〜12時間、中高生8〜10時間が目安。身体活動はこどもで1日平均60分以上（中強度以上）。座りっぱなしの時間、特に余暇のスクリーンタイムを減らす（時間の上限は示されていません）。

Ministry of Education (MEXT) 文部科学省

Early Childhood Physical Activity Guideline (幼児期運動指針, 2012): preschoolers should move their bodies for at least 60 minutes in total each day, mainly through play and including active play at home, not only at the preschool. The goal is a wide range of movements, not a training program.

Generative AI guidelines for schools (初等中等教育段階における生成AIの利活用に関するガイドライン): an interim version came out in July 2023, and Ver. 2.0 in December 2024. The core ideas are human-centered use (the person remains responsible for the final judgment) and strengthening information literacy in light of AI. For students' learning use, schools are asked to consider the child's developmental stage and information literacy and to put risk measures in place, and everyone should follow the service's terms of use. Your school or board of education may have its own rules, so check with the school.

日本語：幼児は毎日合計60分以上、遊びを中心に体を動かす（幼児期運動指針）。生成AIは「人間中心」で、児童生徒の利用は発達段階やリスク対策を踏まえ、利用規約を守る。学校・教育委員会の方針も確認を。

Children and Families Agency こども家庭庁

Each year the agency runs the Survey on Youth Internet Use (青少年のインターネット利用環境実態調査). It has found that internet use starts early, rising with age through the preschool years, and that most parents know about filtering but fewer use it. In recent preliminary results, filtering use was about 54% among parents of young people and about 28% among parents of younger children. The agency publishes parent-facing leaflets and materials (普及啓発コンテンツ). Under the Youth Internet Environment Act (青少年インターネット環境整備法) providers must offer filtering, and guardians are asked to help children use the internet appropriately, which in practice means turning the settings on and setting family rules.

日本語：フィルタリングは「知っている」保護者が約9割でも、「使っている」割合は低い（低年齢層の保護者で約3割）。端末・回線・アプリの設定と家庭のルールづくりを。

Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications (MIC) 総務省 · e-ネットキャラバン

e-Net Caravan (e-ネットキャラバン) offers free internet-safety talks for children (from about grade 3), parents and teachers, run by the FMMC with support from MIC and MEXT. Schools and groups apply for a lecturer (at least 15 participants). If you are on a PTA or parent committee, it is a simple way to bring a talk to your school. MIC also runs the Illegal & Harmful Information Consultation Center (違法・有害情報相談センター), which advises on asking sites to remove harmful posts.

National Consumer Affairs Center 国民生活センター

The center has documented cases of children running up large charges in online games using a parent's registered card or phone, sometimes after choosing "20 or older" at an age prompt. Prevention tips: don't let children use a parent's account, require a password for every purchase, and check statements. A purchase by a minor without a parent's consent can sometimes be cancelled, but it is not guaranteed, especially if the child's age was misstated. The consumer hotline is 188.

Help lines in Japan 日本の相談窓口

🚨 In an emergency, call 110 (police) or 119 (fire and ambulance). Numbers and hours can change and some IP phones cannot connect; check the linked page before relying on one.
