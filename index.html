Four ideas that show up in almost every expert source

  1. Connection beats control. Kids tell you more when they expect calm, not punishment.
  2. Quality, context and what it replaces matter more than a single magic number of minutes.
  3. Co-use early. Watch, play and explore with your child, then give independence in steps.
  4. Plans beat arguments. Agree on routines in calm moments, and revisit them as kids grow.

🤖 Trail 1

AI in Learning & Life

Your child will grow up with AI in their search tools, homework apps and games. The goal isn't to fear it or to hand over the keys — it's to build understanding, curiosity and good judgment.

Big ideas

What AI actually is (say it to your child like this)

Most of today's AI finds patterns in huge amounts of data and uses them to predict or generate something — a recommended video, a face match, the next word in a sentence. Chatbots are trained to produce text that sounds right. That's different from knowing, understanding or caring.

The AI4K12 initiative (AAAI & CSTA) frames school-age AI literacy around five big ideas: perception, representation & reasoning, learning, natural interaction, and societal impact. Elementary kids can explore all five through play.

Confident doesn't mean correct

Generative AI can state wrong facts — or invent books, quotes and links — in a calm, authoritative voice. Build one habit early: "Who says so, and how can we check?" Try the Fact-check Lab below.

Humans stay in charge

The U.S. Department of Education's 2023 report on AI and teaching centers on keeping humans in the loop — teachers, parents and students making the decisions, with AI as a tool. UNESCO's 2023 guidance on generative AI similarly stresses a human-centered approach, teacher involvement, and protecting learners' data and wellbeing.

Homework: learning is the point

The test for any AI use: "Am I still doing the thinking?" Explaining, quizzing and brainstorming can support learning. Having AI produce the work to hand in replaces it. Schools differ — check your school's AI policy and keep teachers in the loop.

Privacy & age limits

Anything typed or uploaded may be stored or used by the service. Teach "no names, school, address, photos of people, or secrets." Many general-purpose chatbots set a minimum age of 13 in their terms (some require a parent's permission under 18), and UNESCO noted 13 as a commonly used threshold while saying many experts think it's too young. Read each tool's terms; supervised, child-specific tools are a better fit for younger kids.

AI "friends" and companions

Common Sense Media's risk assessment of AI companion apps concluded they are not suitable for anyone under 18, because they can produce inappropriate content, are built to be agreeable, and aren't equipped for serious emotional conversations. Keep your child's emotional support human: parents, family, teachers, counselors.

Fakes, bias and "is this real?"

AI can create realistic images, voices and video, and it can reflect biases in its training data. For elementary kids: "Pictures and videos can be edited or made by computers. If it's surprising, pause and ask."

⚖️ Trail 2

Media Balance

Screens aren't all good or all bad. The question is what the screen is doing for your child — and what it's pushing out.

Big ideas

From "how many minutes?" to "how, what and when?"

The American Academy of Pediatrics' January 2026 policy statement, Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents, moves beyond simple screen-time limits. It looks at platform design (many are built to maximize engagement and profit), the quality of content, the relationships around use, and what media displaces. It calls for safer defaults like autoplay off and no targeted ads to minors — and still encourages families to make a plan.

Protect the big three: sleep, movement, people

Guidelines suggest roughly 9–11 hours of sleep for ages 5–13 (Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines; the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's consensus is 9–12 hours for ages 6–12), about 60 minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous activity (Canada, WHO), and — in Canada's and Australia's guidelines — no more than about 2 hours of recreational screen time per day. Treat that last number as a reference point, not a verdict; the displacement question matters more.

The "5 Cs" check

A framework popularized by pediatrician Dr. Jenny Radesky suggests asking: Child (how is mine doing?), Content (is it good?), Calm (can they stop without a meltdown?), Crowding out (what's it replacing?), and Communication (are we talking about it?). Try the Quality Check below.

Bedtime and bedrooms

The AAP has long recommended keeping devices out of bedrooms overnight and turning screens off about an hour before bed. Charging devices in a shared space makes that easier for everyone — grown-ups included.

Design tricks that make stopping hard

Autoplay, streaks, infinite scroll and reward loops are designed to keep people watching. Telling kids "this was built to be hard to stop" turns a power struggle into a shared puzzle. The U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory on social media and youth mental health makes the same point about teens' use; elementary years are the time to build awareness before the pull gets stronger.

Model it

Kids notice when a phone comes out at dinner. Agree on grown-up screen-free times too — it's fairer, and it works better.

🛡️ Trail 3

Internet Safety

Tools help, but the strongest safety feature is a child who knows they can tell you anything and won't lose the device — or your trust — for doing so.

Big ideas

"Go, Pause, Stop & Tell"

A simple script kids can remember: Go = fine. Pause = check with a grown-up first. Stop & tell = don't engage, leave or close it, tell a trusted adult. Practice it with the game below.

Tell-us-and-you're-not-in-trouble

NCMEC (the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children), through its NetSmartz program, and StopBullying.gov both stress responding calmly and making sure children know it isn't their fault. If kids fear losing devices, they hide problems. Say it out loud, often: "If something online feels weird, tell me. You will not be in trouble."

Where kids actually meet strangers

It's not only social media. Multiplayer game chat, comment sections, and shared creative platforms let unknown people reach children. Look at who can message your child and set chat to friends-only or off where possible.

Age minimums and privacy law

In the U.S., the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) limits how online services collect personal information from children under 13, which is a main reason most big social platforms set 13 as their minimum age. Age checks are easy to bypass, so don't rely on them — treat the minimum as guidance and decide as a family.

Scams, secrets and "special friends"

Warning signs worth teaching: requests for personal info, offers that are "free," pressure to move to another app, asking for pictures, and any request to keep a secret from parents. Online enticement and sextortion are most often reported with teens, but the groundwork — body privacy, "no secrets from parents," telling early — starts in the elementary years (see NCMEC's sextortion tip sheet for parents).

Kindness & cyberbullying

StopBullying.gov's advice: don't retaliate, save evidence (screenshots), block and report, and tell a trusted adult or the school. Ask "How would it feel to get this message?" before sending.

Parental controls: useful, not magic

Built-in tools like Apple Screen Time, Google Family Link and Microsoft Family Safety can set content ratings, approve downloads and manage time. Kids find workarounds, so pair them with conversation and shared spaces.
